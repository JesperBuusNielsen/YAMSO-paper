\section{An Informal YAMSO Model}
\label{sec:model}

We first describe the model at the level needed to understand the
construction. Formal real and ideal executions will be added after the
remaining choices in \cref{sec:preliminaries} have been fixed.

\subsection{Machines and the ledger}

For a security parameter $\lambda$, let $\mathcal{P}=[N]$ be a finite set of
eligible machines. The ledger contains an anonymous public record for each
machine. Each machine has the corresponding secret decryption capability,
but the public records do not reveal which machines have been assigned
protocol roles. The ledger totally orders all accepted messages and provides
first-write-wins slots for inputs and protocol activations.

An ordinary machine may speak at most once during its lifetime. When it
speaks, its prescribed message is appended to the ledger and cannot be
dropped. Immediately afterwards its complete state is revealed to the
adversary. This automatic leakage only strengthens the adversary and avoids
having to distinguish between messages and the states that computed them.
The machine is never used again. A machine that never speaks need not reveal
its state or even the fact that it holds a role.

\subsection{Hidden assignment and corruption}

The adversary may choose and inspect up to $N/4$ machines. Its choice need
not be random and may depend on everything learned so far. Roles, however,
are placed independently and uniformly among machines whose public appearance
does not reveal the assignment. Conditional on the current view, unopened
machines must remain exchangeable. In particular, corrupting a committee
member must not reveal any linkage to its peers.

Let $C$ be a hidden committee of size $k$, and let $X$ be the number of its
members among an adversarially selected quarter of the population. Then $X$
is hypergeometric with mean at most $k/4$. Hoeffding's inequality for
sampling without replacement gives
\[
  \Pr[X\geq k/2] \leq \exp(-k/8)
  \quad\text{and}\quad
  \Pr[X\geq k/3] \leq \exp(-k/72).
\]
Thus an appropriate logarithmic committee size makes the probability of a
bad committee negligible across any polynomial number of realized roles.
The randomness here is in the hidden role placement, not in the adversary's
choice of whom to corrupt.

Once a committee speaks, the identities and states of all its members may be
treated as public, independently of the quarter budget. This does not affect
the message already accepted by the ledger. It is harmless only if the
speaking machines have no role in an unselected sibling committee or any
future epoch.

\subsection{Input providers and intended security}

A strong input provider $I$ is permitted to retain private state. For each
input position it will receive two random activation tokens and their semantic
mapping. It posts only the token representing its chosen input. If $I$ is
later corrupted, its input and full state may be revealed and the simulator
is given the input. If $I$ remains honest and uncorrupted, the unused token
and the input bit must remain hidden.

Informally, security should say that the adversary learns the public output,
the state of every ordinary machine that speaks, the complete state and input
of each corrupted input provider, and the state of every machine it corrupts
within its global budget---but nothing else. In particular it should not
learn an unselected garbled label or identify enough members of a silent
committee to reconstruct it.

The first formalization should distinguish privacy from availability. A
corrupt selected committee may withhold or malformedly publish shares unless
the threshold scheme and ledger specify recovery and verification. It must
also specify whether the machine population grows between epochs and how the
global quarter budget is measured in that case.
