\subsection{Projective Garbling}
\label{sec:garbling}

Bellare, Hoang, and Rogaway formalized garbling as a cryptographic
primitive~\cite{BellareHoangRogaway2012Foundations}.  We use the syntax of
Hemenway et al.~\cite[full version, Definition~1]{HemenwayEtAl2016AdaptiveGarbling}.
For a circuit $C\colon\bits^n\to\bits^m$, sample
$(\GC,e)\sample\Garble(1^\lambda,C)$.  Publish $\GC$ and keep the
encoding key $e$ private.  Projectivity gives two labels $L_{w,0},L_{w,1}$
per input wire $w$ and a common string $\GarbleAux$, possibly empty.
The encoded input and evaluation are
\[
  X\gets\Encode(e,x)=(\GarbleAux,L_{1,x_1},\ldots,L_{n,x_n}),
  \qquad y\gets\Eval(\GC,X).
\]
Both algorithms are deterministic, and $y\in\bits^m\cup\{\bot\}$.
The common string is released with the labels.  We require a sampled garbling to evaluate correctly on every input
simultaneously except with negligible probability.  This strengthens the
fixed-input correctness clause of Hemenway et al. and covers inputs
chosen after $\GC$ is public.

For \emph{adaptive privacy}, the simulator first produces the garbling
using only the wiring and designated input and output wires, denoted
$\Topo(C)$.  After the adversary chooses $x$ on seeing $\GC$, the
simulator produces one encoding from the output alone.  The calls are
\[
  (\GC,\tau)\sample\SimGarble(1^\lambda,\Topo(C)),
  \qquad X\sample\SimEncode(\tau,C(x)).
\]
The two replies are jointly indistinguishable from the real replies.
The projective scheme of Hemenway et al. achieves these properties from
one-way functions.  Appendix~\ref{app:garbling} gives the game and the
all-input correctness argument.
